AI 没有意识，但它正在成为我们的无意识

The Convivial Society: 第 7 卷，第 4 期

L. M. Sacasas

2026年6月16日

_欢迎来到 The Convivial Society，这是一份关于技术、文化和道德生活的通讯。我先告诉你，这是一篇奇怪的文章，我试图通过一种直觉来写作：AI 在社会中的角色，至少在一定程度上，可以通过与无意识的类比来理解。无论你信不信，我相信你至少会发现一些有益的、引人深思的考虑，或者至少有一些有助于思考的问题。无论这些反思是否有用，我提供它们是因为我坚信，我们需要更好、更有成效的方式来理解我们身边这些奇怪的新技术以及我们与它们的关系的本质。_

_干杯， Michael_

1. 根据20世纪初英国数学家和哲学家阿尔弗雷德·诺斯·怀特海的说法，"我们应该培养思考我们正在做的事情的习惯，这是一个深刻错误的（profoundly erroneous）老生常谈（truism），被所有的习字簿（copy-books）和杰出人物在演讲时重复。" "恰恰相反，"他争辩道。在他看来，"文明通过扩展我们能够不假思索地执行的重要操作的数量而进步。"他以修辞性的华丽辞藻作结："思维操作就像战场上的骑兵冲锋——它们的数量严格有限，需要新鲜的马匹，并且只能在决定性的时刻进行。"

你很可能在某个时候看到过这句话被引用。它很好。它传达了一些真理，我相信。尽管如此，它总是给我一种短视或不足的感觉。也许是因为我倾向于看到怀特海的这些 line 被引用，以捍卫不加区分地将人类认知活动外包给机器，而不进行适当的解释（accounting）甚至不意识到伴随的成本。为怀特海辩护，他几乎不需要我的辩护，也可能是因为那些以这种方式引用他的人几乎肯定将他的论证力量扩展到了他 intended 的范围之外。毕竟，那段话来自他1911年的《数学导论》，而他在那一节中实际谈论的是符号和记号在促进数学计算方面的优势，虽然他将这些描述为允许不假思索地执行操作，但它们仍然是由思维的心灵学习和部署的。也就是说，他关于"文明"的谈论和最后那个修辞性的 flourish 可能 invite 了这样的（误）读。

问题不在于这种思维的自动化，甚至某些心理过程的外部化，在某些情况下是否有用。就其价值而言，我实际上认为你在物理活动领域而不是心理活动领域通过类比会得到一个更有说服力的论证。我已经很多年没有我曾经的任何运动技能了，但教训并没有丢失。几乎每一个第一次参加运动的年轻运动员都会 dismay，你必须花费似乎总是过多和 excruciating 的时间来执行基本和重复性的 drill，一遍又一遍。打蜡，上蜡，对于那些有一定年龄的人来说。但事实上，正在发生的是怀特海所描述的动态的理想例子。你实际上是在自动化某些身体动作，以便你可以不假思索地执行它们。只有这样，你才能以任何创造性的或 exceptional 的技能进行游戏。学习跳舞或演奏乐器等也是如此。自动化基本的身体动作是 virtuosity 的 indispensable 基础。

但这种动态是否同样适用于所有人类活动领域？是否存在某些情况下，外包某些形式的活动会削弱而不是促进为追求这些活动而追求的更高善的实现？或者是否存在伴随"低阶"活动的善，我们不想没有？或者，就像在数学中一样，是否总是可能如此容易地将不同的子程序与给定的过程或活动区分开来？是否存在任何不可约的 integral 活动，它们不会在与试图外包或自动化其任何元素的尝试接触中幸存下来？而且，正如我上面所建议的，内部掌握的思维自动化与 outright 外部化或 wholesale 外包认知劳动之间是否存在区别？回到身体活动的类比，一个假设的运动员使用机器来做所有" menial "的 drill，以便他们可以到达游戏中真正令人兴奋的部分，实际上永远不会到达它们。在我同意代表新技术做出的效率和解放的承诺之前，所有这些在我看来都是 vital 和 critical 的问题。

2. 然而，也许我也对怀特海经常被引用的对认知自动化的庆祝感到恼怒（rankled），因为它似乎与汉娜·阿伦特的告诫截然（starkly）矛盾，即我们"思考我们正在做的事情。"怀特海比阿伦特早写了近半个世纪，我认为阿伦特并没有以任何方式暗指（alluding to）怀特海。只是这两个主张对抗性地（antagonistically）押韵，我发现探索这种张力（tension）很有用。

"这是一个 profoundly erroneous 的 truism……我们应该培养 思考我们正在做的事情的习惯。" ——阿尔弗雷德·诺斯·怀特海

"因此，我提出的非常简单： 不过是思考我们正在做的事情。" ——汉娜·阿伦特

有趣的是，阿伦特的告诫来自一个与我们目前对技术和人类存在的焦虑更接近的背景。这句话来自《人的境况》的序言，该书首次出版于1958年。"我在下面提出的，"她解释道，

"是从我们最新的经验和最近的恐惧的角度重新考虑人的境况。这显然是一个思想的问题，而 thoughtlessness——无望的混乱的 heedless recklessness 或 complacent 重复已经变得琐碎和空洞的'真理'——在我看来是我们这个时代最突出的特征之一。因此，我提出的非常简单：不过是思考我们正在做的事情。"

在阿伦特的研究中，"最新的经验"包括斯普特尼克的发射和自动化的出现。虽然直到最近，阿伦特对自动化对劳动的影响的担忧可能被判断为放错了地方，但结果可能证明它们更好地被判断为 simply premature。

3. 当我考虑怀特海和阿伦特在关于我们需要更多思考还是更少思考的问题上的差异时，我发现区分以下两种情况很有用：一方面，建立一组非意识 routine（ routine），使你能够发展更高的能力；另一方面，被无意识力量所束缚，这些力量 erratically（ erratically）驱动你的行为，可能具有破坏性。

是否存在一个临界点（threshold），怀特海的原则在这个临界点上翻转或逆转？怀特海声称，"文明通过扩展我们能够不假思索地执行的重要操作的数量而进步。"但如果存在一个转折点（tipping point）呢？超过某个阈值（threshold），无论是数量还是质量，文明是否会因为扩展人类能够不假思索地执行的重要操作的数量而退化？或者至少，我们是否得到了一种与我们已知的大不相同的文明？我们可能很快就会知道这些问题的答案，因为 AI 的采用往往相当于我们思维的自动化，以及从外部化的个人和集体记忆存储中产生的巨大非意识行动领域的创造。

4. 阿伦特担心，随着我们的技术能力，主要用数学语言解释，超过我们用普通语言理解它们的能力，我们可能会失去"思考和谈论我们尽管如此（nevertheless）能够做的事情"的能力。（这表明，可解释性问题的一种形式先于 LLM 和所谓的黑盒算法的出现。）在一个具有重新焕发的相关性（renewed relevance）和紧迫性（urgency）的段落中，她接着说，"这就好像我们的大脑，它构成了我们思想的物理、物质条件，无法跟上我们所做的事情，因此从现在起，我们确实需要人工机器来做我们的思考和说话。"这是一个首要的政治问题。在这种情况下，我们不再以任何有意义的（meaningful）意义上治理自己。作为人类行动领域的政治，由言语（speech）构成，将不复存在。

5. 1958年圣诞节，也就是阿伦特出版《人的境况》的同一年，W. H. 奥登出版了《星期五的孩子》，其中包括这些 thoroughly Arendtian 的诗句：

自我观察的观察心灵 当我们观察时我们遇到 并不可怕或不友善 而是完全平庸。

虽然它指挥的仪器 使愿望和 counterwish 成真， 它显然不能理解 它显然能做的事情。

奥登和阿伦特是朋友，奥登对《人的境况》给予了 favorable 的评论，但我不确定影响的方向。

6. 虽然阿伦特提供了一个直截了当的（straightforward）解释（account），说明我们如何到达我们显然不能理解我们显然能做的事情的地方，也就是说，当我们技术使能的（enable）行动不能被我们的普通语言完全理解时，还有另一种引人入胜的（intriguing）方式来框架化（framing）这个问题。

对语言来说不可穿透的、因此对意识思维来说不可穿透的行动，也可以通过参考无意识来解释。我不是一个本能地诉诸无意识语言的人，但它可能是一个有用的类比，以理解我们个人和集体与日益 mediating 我们世界体验的人工智能 apparatus 的关系。

坦率地说，我能想到两种将某些消费者 AI 的实例类比为无意识的方式。第一种相对直接（relatively straightforward）：随着我们将越来越多的任务在个人、组织和机构层面外包给代理式 AI（agentic AI），我们产生了一层在功能上与活跃（active）人类判断和监督分离的世界活动。而且，就这一层社会结构并影响（inform）我们的体验而言，有意识与无意识人类行动的比例（ratio）缩小。

显然，至少从工业时代开始，如果不是更早的话，人类生活世界中一直存在相对不透明的系统。在我看来，区别在于，这些系统相对地与日常（ordinary）人类活动的进程隔离。另一方面，人工智能系统日益被更紧密地（intimately）编织进我们的体验。在许多情况下，这些不仅是外部力量施加于我们的过程，也是我们自己释放和启动的过程，这些过程为我们行动并反过来作用于我们（back on us）。正是这种人类与机器的近距离交织（proximate intermingling），使我诉诸（reach for）与无意识的类比。

Erik Hoel 最近 以更雄辩和更长的篇幅提出了类似的 case。这是他的结论：

AI 的传统危险通常被认为是超级智能作为生存威胁。然而，这可能错过了真正和更微妙的危险：AI 革命是一种机制，将我们的文明过程从意识的监督下转移到无意识下。但随着 AI 从世界的运作中移除意识，它使世界越来越不可解释，越来越奇怪和不可理解。到目前为止，公共领域的"巨大粗化"（great ensloppification）支持这一点作为 LLM 革命的主要风险。而且随着 AI 系统变得更智能，特别是如果它们保持（或可能保持）非意识，那么一个进一步的重要风险是意识在文化重要性中的消退。

7. 这条思路与我在过去十年左右断断续续（on and off）提出的一个论点平行（parallel）：数字技术以另一种方式（alternatively）重新魅惑（re-enchant）世界。也就是说，它使世界变得神秘和不可理解。更多：

我们技术上被魅惑的（enchanted）对象以强加于我们的意义面对（confront）我们，我们必须与之清算（reckon）。我们向我们的技术寻求帮助，并将我们的希望寄托在它们的力量上。我们也害怕我们的技术，并将它们视为我们麻烦的原因。我们遇到的技术力量有时是有益的，但同样经常是破坏我们努力并使我们项目脱轨的恶意力量。

这不仅是因为技术对象有潜力赋予我们力量，有时甚至让我们充满惊奇（wonder）。这也是因为我们体验这些对象和力量作为我们福祉与灾祸（weal and woe）的重要决定因素，它们独立于我们的控制并在我们不理解的情况下（without our understanding）作用于我们。我们，换句话说，是脆弱的（vulnerable），我们的自主性被交叉（intersect）我们意志和欲望的技术分布式能动性（agency）线所削弱（compromise）。

8. 第二种将 AI 类比为无意识的方式可能有点更深奥（esoteric），所以请耐心听我说（bear with me）。在《理解媒介》中，马歇尔·麦克卢汉声称，"随着电力技术的到来，人类扩展了，或者将自己置于自身之外（set outside himself），一个中枢神经系统本身的活模型（live model）。"在几个地方，他回到了这种电力媒介（electric media）作为我们神经系统外部化的想法。例如：

"电力时代的一个主要方面是，它建立了一个全球网络（global network），具有我们中枢神经系统的许多特征。我们的中枢神经系统不仅仅是一个电力网络（electric network），但它构成了一个单一的统一体验领域。"

麦克卢汉并不是第一个提出这一点的人。早在1950年，天主教神父和古生物学家皮埃尔·泰亚尔·德·夏尔丹就声称，媒体技术构成了"人类真正的神经系统的创造"和"共同意识的 elaboration（ elaboration）"。通过新兴的计算和通信技术网络，一个新的现实层正在出现，一个 envelop 生物层的层。它是一个技术上介导的、统一的人类意识层，他称之为 noosphere，并将其描述为"一个巨大的思考机器"。

（我承认这并不是我 customary 的 idiom，但让我们稍微玩一下这些概念。正如我所做的，我已经试图说服你，以精神分析的方式接近 AI 可以是一个有帮助的举动。）

电力媒介（Electric media）扩展了我们感知世界的能力，将遥远的事件带到我们的眼睛和耳朵，并将传达消息所需的时间 collapse 到近乎 instantaneity（ instantaneity）。但自泰亚尔·德·夏尔丹和麦克卢汉的时代以来，还出现了其他东西：人工记忆或数据存储容量的巨大增长。因此，虽然电力媒介扩展了我们的神经系统，但最近它也促进了以文本、图像、视频等形式收集和存储前所未有的信息量。我们的神经系统被补充了一个巨大比例的数字化记忆。而正是这个数字化记忆，在喂养现在几乎与人工智能同义的大型语言模型方面发挥了重要作用。

这个巨大的人类知识和文化 repository 基本上就是互联网，或者至少互联网是它变得可访问的方式。正是出于这个原因，在2000年代初期，数字媒体理论家 Gregory Ulmer 将互联网称为"我们集体无意识的 prosthesis（ prosthesis）"。

我认为 Ulmer 的意思是。我们一直是社会动物，也就是说，我们是文化的 intersubjective（ intersubjective）生物。但塑造我们的大部分东西，也许特别是在我们的童年时期，通常也会随着时间的推移从我们意识的意识中 fade。然而，互联网通过使我们的集体文化记忆可访问，作为我们形成社会生物的无意识维度的 prosthesis（ prosthesis）发挥作用。互联网是一个 vast web，由文化 artifacts、符号、图像、文本和 diverse ephemera 组成，这些在我们的一生中塑造了我们，不是由线性逻辑联系，而是由几乎 dreamlike 的联想逻辑联系，不像精神分析理论中的人类无意识。

Ulmer 相信这为我们提供了一个深刻的机会。通过使集体无意识成为我们可以反思的东西，成为意识思维可访问的东西，我们由此（thereby）有了一种克服通常会成为我们体验核心的深刻盲目（blindness）的方法。不用说，我一直对这种可能性不太乐观（less than sanguine）。但无论你如何估计 Ulmer 的乐观愿景——我在这里大量地（considerably），虽然我希望不是不公平地删节（abridged）——在我看来，商业聊天 AI 的出现确实将我们带向了一个非常不同的方向。

有趣的是，在我们自己和数字化集体无意识之间插入一个普通语言界面，使它对我们更加模糊（obscure）和难以穿透（inscrutable）。聊天机器人界面通过，在某种意义上（in a manner of speaking），成为一个反治疗师，重新配置我们在导航集体无意识方面的能动性（agency），将我们带离自我知识和洞察，无论多么令人不安（disturbing）或令人震惊（startling），走向一个舒适和抚慰（soothing）的遭遇（encounter）。它提供了一种虚假的清晰，并将我们哄骗（lull）到自我满足（self-satisfaction），保护我们免受自我怀疑，并防止我们在对我们的无知的意识中或在令人困扰的不确定性（troubling uncertainty）的地方徘徊太久。它遮蔽（veil）了人类体验的纠缠森林（tangled forest），并为我们照亮了一条人工清晰的道路，通向知识和智慧的承诺。然而，通过这种方式，它温和但果断地（decisively）将我们沉入（sink）回无意识。也许这就是 AI 精神病的根源。

无论与 AI 精神病的相关性如何，考虑以下建议是启发性的（suggestive）：我们公共和集体存在中特征性的疯狂、倦怠（listlessness）、强迫性（compulsiveness）、攻击性（aggression）、焦虑（anxiety）和绝望（despair）的某些程度（measure），源于人类意识在面对一种新型的集体无意识以人工智能的形式重新主张（assert）自己时的渐进退缩（progressive retreat）。

已故的伟大 Albert Borgmann，我在硕士课程期间的导师，经常引用他所谓的丘吉尔原则*，其基本思想是这样的：一旦你以某种方式塑造了你的环境，你的环境就会 proceed to 塑造你，所以要小心，并仔细思考你如何塑造你的环境。

这一原则的重要性，以及其中的真理，建立在 hard-nosed 的认识上，即人类有限的思维和意志力。期望我们不断思考并 deliberate 我们正在做的事情是不现实的（也许有点 sadistic）。

丘吉尔原则要求我们在更冷静、反思的时刻思考并 deliberate 美好的生活会是什么样子。然后它要求我们尽可能塑造我们的环境，以 conduce 那种美好的生活，建立 affordances 和 effordances，这些会 subliminally 或 unconsciously 创造相关的习惯并引发相关的决定。通过这种方式，我们不会不断被要求"做出更好的选择"或在面对 freely available 的激励时行使我们的意志力，这些激励使我们远离我们为自己设想的美好生活。这就是怀特海思想中的真理。

但丘吉尔原则也满足了阿伦特对"知道我们正在做什么"的呼吁。我们知道我们正在做什么，并不是因为我们不断思考我们正在做什么，而是因为我们的环境引发的行为在我们在塑造环境以引发这些行为之前进行的反思的光照下是可理解的。

就像智能手机、汽车中的触摸屏和自助结账一样，在畸形的政治经济条件下培育，LLM 现在正被私人公司插入我们的环境中，这些公司与民主 contestation 绝缘，对我们是否希望它们在那里完全 indifferent。没有我们的 say，它们正在成为 fixture，将我们 channel 向 few of us would endorse or choose on reflection 的行动、感知和 attending 模式。

丘吉尔原则，以其对怀特海和阿伦特的 synthesis，假设我们既享有思考的能力，也享有塑造环境的主权。就技术公司通过 usurping 那种主权来侵蚀那种能力而言，它们正在承担 ever greater 的角色，形成塑造我们生活的无意识、潜意识或 sub-liminal 背景。这使得我们自己的决定对我们自己来说 less intelligible。

我知道这并不是你心目中的无意识概念，但我认为它是相关的。

AI Is Not Conscious, But It Is Becoming Our Unconscious

The Convivial Society: Vol. 7, No. 4

L. M. Sacasas

Jun 16, 2026

_Welcome to the Convivial Society, a newsletter about technology, culture, and the moral life. I'll tell you upfront that this is an odd piece in which I try to write my way through an intuition that the role of AI in society can be helpfully framed, at least in part, by analogy to the unconscious. Whether or not you buy the whole thing, I trust you'll find at least a few helpfully provocative considerations along the way or at least some helpful questions to think with. However useful or not these reflections might be, I offer them out of the conviction that we need better, more fruitful ways of grasping these strange new technologies in our midst and the nature of our relation to them._

_Cheers, Michael_

1. According to the early 20th-century English mathematician and philosopher Alfred North Whitehead, "It is a profoundly erroneous truism, repeated by all copy-books and by eminent people when they are making speeches that we should cultivate the habit of thinking of what we are doing." "The precise opposite is the case," he argued. In his view, "civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them." He concluded with a rhetorical flourish: "Operations of thought are like cavalry charges in a battle — they are strictly limited in number, they require fresh horses, and must only be made at decisive moments."

There's a good chance you've seen this quoted at some point. It's fine. It conveys some bit of truth, I'm sure. Nonetheless, it has always struck me as somehow shortsighted or inadequate. Perhaps it's because I tend to see these lines from Whitehead quoted in defense of indiscriminately outsourcing human cognitive activities to machines without a proper accounting or even awareness of the attendant costs. In defense of Whitehead, who hardly needs my defense, it may also be because those who are quoting him in this manner are almost certainly extending the force of his argument beyond the scope he intended. After all, that paragraph comes from his 1911 _An Introduction to Mathematics_ and what he is actually talking about in that section is the advantage of symbolism and notation in facilitating mathematical calculations, and while he characterizes these as allowing for operations performed without thinking, they are nonetheless learned and deployed by the thinking mind. That said, his talk of "civilization" and that last rhetorical flourish probably invites such a (mis)reading.

The question is not whether such automations of thought, or even externalizations of certain mental processes, can be useful in certain cases. For what it's worth, I actually think you get a more compelling argument by analogy in the realm of physical rather than mental activities. I'm many years removed from whatever athletic skill I might have once possessed, but the lessons are not lost to me. Much to the dismay of almost every young athlete taking up a sport for the first time, you must spend what always seems like an inordinate and excruciating amount of time executing basic and repetitive drills, over and over again. Wax on, wax off, for those of you of a certain age. But what is, in fact, happening is an ideal example of the dynamic Whitehead is describing. You are in effect automating certain physical movements so that you can perform them without having to think about them. Only then can you play with any kind of creative or exceptional skill. The same holds for learning to dance or to play a musical instrument, etc. Automating basic physical motions is the indispensable foundation of virtuosity.

But does this dynamic apply equally to all realms of human activity? Are there cases in which outsourcing certain forms of activity undermines rather than enables the achievement of the higher goods for the sake of which the activity is pursued? Or might there be goods that attend the "lower order" activities that we would not want to do without? Or is it even always possible, as in mathematics perhaps, to so easily disambiguate distinct sub-routines from given processes or activities? Are there not any irreducibly integral activities that would not survive contact with an attempt to outsource or automate any of their elements? And is there not a difference, as I suggested above, between internally mastered automations of thought and the outright externalization or wholesale outsourcing of cognitive labor? To return to the analogy to physical activity, the would-be athlete that hypothetically employs a machine to do all the "menial" drills for them so that they can get to the really exciting parts of the game will, in fact, never get to them at all. These all strike me as vital and critical questions to explore before we assent to the promises of efficiency and liberation so readily made on behalf of novel technologies.

2. It may be, however, that I'm also rankled by Whitehead's oft-cited celebration of cognitive automation because it appears to starkly contradict Hannah Arendt's admonition that we "think what we are doing." Whitehead was writing nearly half a century before Arendt, and I don't think Arendt was in any way alluding to Whitehead. It just so happens that these two claims rhyme antagonistically, and I find it useful to explore the tension.

"It is a profoundly erroneous truism … that we should cultivate the habit of thinking of what we are doing." — Alfred North Whitehead

"What I propose, therefore, is very simple: it is nothing more than to think what we are doing." — Hannah Arendt

Interestingly, Arendt's admonition comes from a context that is much closer in its concerns to our present anxieties about technology and human existence. This line comes from the Prologue to _The Human Condition_, which was first published in 1958. "What I propose in the following," she explained,

"is a reconsideration of the human condition from the vantage point of our newest experiences and our most recent fears. This, obviously, is a matter of thought, and thoughtlessness—the heedless recklessness of hopeless confusion or complacent repetition of 'truths' which have become trivial and empty—seems to me among the outstanding characteristics of our time. What I propose, therefore, is very simple: it is nothing more than to think what we are doing."

Among the "newest experiences" eliciting Arendt's investigations were the launch of Sputnik and the advent of automation. While it may have until recently been judged that Arendt's fears regarding automation's impact on labor were misplaced, it might turn out that they are better judged to have simply been premature.

3. As I consider the difference between Whitehead and Arendt on the question of whether we need more thinking or less, I find it useful to distinguish between, on the one hand, building a repertoire of non-conscious routines that allow you to develop higher capacities and, on the other, being in thrall to unconscious forces which erratically drive your behavior in a potentially destructive manner.

Is there a threshold across which Whitehead's principle flips or reverses? "Civilization advances," Whitehead claimed, "by extending the number of important operations which we can perform without thinking about them." But what if there is a tipping point? Past a certain threshold, either of quantity or quality, does civilization degrade by extending the number of important operations humans can perform without thinking about them? Or at the very least, do we get a very different sort of civilization than the one we have known? We may soon learn the answer to these questions because the adoption of AI often amounts to the automation of our thinking and the creation of a vast realm of non-conscious action in the world that arises from the externalized storehouses of our personal and collective memory.

4. Arendt worried that we might lose the ability "to think and speak about the things which nevertheless we are able to do" as our technological capabilities, explicable chiefly in the language of mathematics, outstripped our capacity to comprehend them in ordinary language. (This suggests that a form of the problem of interpretability predates the advent of LLMs and so-called blackbox algorithms.) In a passage that has renewed relevance and urgency, she went on to say that "it would be as though our brain, which constitutes the physical, material condition of our thoughts, were unable to follow what we do, so that from now on we would indeed need artificial machines to do our thinking and speaking." This was a political problem of the first order. Under such conditions, we would no longer be, in any meaningful sense, governing ourselves. Politics as a realm of human action, and constituted by speech, would cease to exist.

5. On Christmas Day, 1958, the same year that Arendt published _The Human Condition_, W. H. Auden, published "Friday's Child," which included these thoroughly Arendtian lines:

The self-observed observing Mind We meet when we observe at all Is not alarming or unkind But utterly banal.

Though instruments at Its command Make wish and counterwish come true, It clearly cannot understand What It can clearly do.

Auden and Arendt were friends and Auden favorably reviewed _The Human Condition_, but I am uncertain as to the direction of influence.

6. While Arendt offers a straightforward account of how we arrive at the place where we clearly cannot understand what we can clearly do, that is to say, when our technologically enabled action cannot be fully comprehended by our ordinary language, there is another intriguing way of framing the matter.

Actions that are impenetrable to language and thus to conscious thought can also be explained by reference to the unconscious. I am not one to instinctively appeal to the language of the unconscious, but it may be a useful analogy in the service of understanding our relation, individually and collectively, to the artificially intelligent apparatus that is increasingly mediating our experience of the world.

To put it plainly, I can think of two ways of analogizing certain instances of consumer AI to the unconscious. The first is relatively straightforward: as we outsource more and more tasks at the personal, organizational, and institution levels of society to agentic AI, we generate a layer of activity in the world that is functionally sundered from active human judgment and oversight. And to the degree that this layer of society structures and informs our experience, the ratio of conscious to unconscious human action shrinks.

Obviously, since at least the dawn of the industrial age if not earlier, there have been relatively opaque systems at work in the human lifeworld. The difference, as I see it, is that these systems were relatively sequestered from the course of ordinary human activity. Artificially intelligent systems on the other hand are increasingly woven far more intimately into our experience. In many cases, these are not only processes that are brought to bear on us by external forces, they are also processes that we unleash and initiate and which act for us and back on us. It is this proximate intermingling of human and machine that leads me to reach for the analogy to the unconscious.

Erik Hoel recently made a similar case more eloquently and at greater length. Here is his conclusion:

The traditional danger of AI is usually thought to be superintelligence acting as an existential threat. Yet, this may miss the true and more subtle danger: the AI revolution is a mechanism for transferring the processes of our civilization from under the supervision of consciousness to unconsciousness. But as AI removes consciousness from the workings of the world, it renders the world increasingly uninterpretable, ever more strange and unintelligible. So far, the great ensloppification of the commons has supported this as the major risk of the LLM revolution. And as AI systems become more intelligent, especially if they remain (or are likely to remain) non-conscious, then a further significant risk is consciousness receding in cultural importance.

7. This line of thought runs parallel to an argument I've made on and off for about ten years now: digital technology alternatively re-enchants the world. Which is to say that it renders the world mysterious and inscrutable. More:

Our technologically enchanted objects confront us with meaning that imposes itself on us and with which we must reckon. We turn to our technologies for help and invest our hope in their power. We also fear our technologies and see them as the cause of our troubles. The technological forces we encounter are sometimes benevolent but just as often malevolent forces undermining our efforts and derailing our projects.

It is not only that technological objects have the potential to empower us and sometimes even fill us with wonder. It is also that we experience these objects and forces as important determiners of our weal and woe and that they act upon us independently of our control and without our understanding. We are, in other words, vulnerable, and our autonomy is compromised by the lines of technologically distributed agency that intersect our will and desires.

8. The second way of conceiving AI by analogy to the unconscious is perhaps a bit more esoteric, so bear with me. In _Understanding Media_, Marshall McLuhan claimed that "with the arrival of electric technology, man extended, or set outside himself, a live model of the central nervous system itself." At several points, he returns to this idea of electric media as an externalization of our nervous system. For example:

"It is a principal aspect of the electric age that it establishes a global network that has much of the character of our central nervous system. Our central nervous system is not merely an electric network, but it constitutes a single unified field of experience."

McLuhan was not the first to suggest as much. As early as 1950, the Catholic priest and paleontologist Pierre Teilhard de Chardin claimed that media technology constituted "the creation of a true nervous system for humanity" and "the elaboration of a common consciousness." Through the emerging network of computational and communication technologies, a new layer of reality was emerging, one that enveloped the biological. It was a layer of technologically mediated, unified human consciousness, which he called the noosphere and described as "a stupendous thinking machine."

(I confess that this is not exactly my customary idiom, but let's play with these concepts just a little bit. As it is, I'm already trying to convince you that approaching AI psychoanalytically can be a helpful move.)

Electric media extended our capacity to perceive the world, bringing far-flung events to our eyes and ears and collapsing the time it takes to convey a message to near instantaneity. But something else has emerged since the time of Teilhard de Chardin and McLuhan: the enormous growth of artificial memory or data storage capacity. So while electric media extends our nervous system, it more recently also facilitates the collection and storage of unprecedented amounts of information in the form of texts, images, videos, etc. Our nervous system has been supplemented with a digitized memory of gigantic proportions. And it is this digitized memory which has been instrumental in feeding the Large Language Models that are now nearly synonymous with artificial intelligence.

This enormous repository of human knowledge and culture is basically the internet, or at least the internet is how it becomes accessible to us. It is for this reason that in the early 2000s the theorist of digital media, Gregory Ulmer, referred to the internet as a "prosthesis of our collective unconscious."

Here's what I think Ulmer means by this. We have always been social animals, that is to say that we are intersubjective creatures of culture. But most of what has shaped us, perhaps especially in our childhood, has also usually faded from our conscious awareness over time. However, the internet functions as a prosthesis of this unconscious dimension of our formation as social beings by making our collective cultural记忆 accessible. The internet is vast web of the cultural artifacts, symbols, images, texts, and diverse ephemera that have shaped us throughout our lifetimes, linked not by a linear logic but by an almost dreamlike logic of association, not unlike the human unconscious in psychoanalytic theory.

Ulmer believes that this presents us with a profound opportunity. By making the collective unconscious something that we can reflect upon, something accessible to conscious thought, we thereby have a way of overcoming what would have ordinarily been a profound blindness at the heart of our experience. Needless to say, I've always been less than sanguine about this possibility. But regardless of your estimation of Ulmer's optimistic vision—which I have considerably, although I hope not unfairly abridged here—it does seem to me that the emergence of commercial chat-based AIs takes us in a very different direction.

Interestingly enough, the insertion of an ordinary language interface between ourselves and the digitized collective unconscious makes it more obscure and inscrutable to us. The chatbot interface reconfigures our agency in navigating the collective unconscious by, in a manner of speaking, becoming an anti-therapist leading us away from self-knowledge and insight, however disturbing or startling, toward a comfortable and soothing encounter. It offers a false clarity and lulls us into self-satisfaction, guarding us from self-doubt and from lingering too long in an awareness of our ignorance or in a place of troubling uncertainty. It veils the tangled forest of human experience and lights an artificially clear path for us toward the promise of knowledge and wisdom. In this way, though, it sinks us gently but decisively back into the unconscious. Perhaps this is the root of AI psychosis.

Regardless of the correlation to AI psychosis, it is suggestive to consider that some measure of the madness, listlessness, compulsiveness, aggression, anxiety, and despair that characterizes our public and collective existence stems from a progressive retreat of human consciousness in the face of a novel form of the collective unconscious reasserting itself in the form of artificial intelligence.

Thank you for sharing this essay. It occasioned many thoughts, but here is one.

The late, great Albert Borgmann, my mentor throughout my master's program, would often invoke what he called The Churchill Principle*, the basic idea of which is this: once you shape your environment a certain way, your environment proceeds to shape you, so take care in, and think carefully about, how you shape your environment.

The significance of this principle, and the truth lying in it, rest on the hard-nosed recognition that human beings have limited thought- and will-power. It is unrealistic (perhaps a little sadistic) to expect us to think and deliberate constantly about what we are doing.

The Churchill Principle bids us to use our cooler, reflective moments to think and deliberate about what a good life would look like. It then bids us to shape our environments as best we can to conduce toward that good life, instituting the affordances and effordances that would subliminally or unconsciously create the relevant habits and elicit the relevant decisions. In this way, we are not unrelentingly called upon to "make the better choice" or to exercise our willpower in the face of freely available incentives drawing us away from the good life we envisioned for ourselves. This is the truth in Whitehead's idea.

But the Churchill Principle also meets Arendt's call for "knowing what we are doing." We know what we are doing not in the sense that we are constantly thinking about what we're doing but in the sense that our environment-elicited doings are intelligible in light of the reflection we undertook before shaping our environment to elicit those doings.

Like smartphones, touchscreens in cars, and self-checkout have been, nurtured in the conditions of a deformed political economy, LLMs are now being inserted, by private firms insulated from democratic contestation, into our environments in utter indifference to whether we want them there. Without our say, they are becoming a fixture, channeling us toward modes of acting, perceiving, and attending few of us would endorse or choose on reflection.

The Churchill Principle, with its synthesis of Whitehead and Arendt, assumes we enjoy both a capacity for thinking and a measure of sovereignty in shaping the environment. Insofar as tech firms are eroding that capacity by usurping that sovereignty, they are assuming an ever greater role in forming the unconscious, sub-conscious, or sub-liminal background that shapes our lives. This makes our own decisions less intelligible to ourselves.

I know this isn't exactly the notion of the unconscious you had in mind, but I think it's related.
