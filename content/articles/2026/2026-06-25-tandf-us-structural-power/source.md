## Abstract

The future of global capitalism is tied to the future of the US’s economic dominance. We argue that many commentators greatly exaggerate US economic decline because they rely on outdated conceptualizations of national economic power from before the post-1990s age of globalization. Once we understand how American power has globalized, the virtually insurmountable nature of US hegemony in global capitalism becomes clear, for the foreseeable future. This is partly because of—not despite—China’s capitalist rise and integration into this system. Here we spell out the several distinct mechanisms, or sinews, by which the US sustains its economic dominance, with particular attention to the global concentration of power and wealth in the hands of US corporations, and to the US control over advanced semiconductors. This structural power will likely endure despite Trump’s behaviors in the diplomatic realm of relational power (including wars), as long as there is no rival that can compete economically and counterbalance militarily. Only anti-capitalist forces could threaten capitalism, and since none are currently on the horizon, there is a greater chance of ecocide and interplanetary colonization than the end of global capitalism in this century.

We understand capitalism as a political economic system undergirded by state power and based on (1) production motivated primarily by the search for profit, and (2) capital owned primarily by capitalists, not by workers. From 18 th century merchant capitalism to 19 th century industrial capitalism, from mid-20 th century Roosevelt-Keynesianism to late 20 th century neoliberalism, capitalism has demonstrated a remarkable capacity to shape-shift around those two central features, guided by powerful actors, each shape generating contradictions whose resolution creates new ones. Since 1945 capitalism has expanded across the planet under the guidance of the United States, the first truly global hegemonic state. We argue that the future of global capitalism depends on the ability of the United States to ensure that (most) other states continue to follow the capitalist logic and institutions long established by the US and its western allies to structure economic interactions between states and transnational corporations (TNCs) – which disproportionately benefit the US and western allies.

The US is not just more powerful than other states, but since 1945 qualitatively unique. It has been the only hegemonic state able to sustain and reshape the global capitalist system in a historically contingent process, beyond the force of “secular stagnation” or other economic causes.

In the first year of the second Trump administration (starting January 2025) its critics claim that Trump’s bravado in promising to revive America’s global power (Make America Great Again, MAGA) merely masks US decline as the global leader. They certainly have a lot of bravado to draw on, as Trump flip-flops on his global tariff war, vows to be the “Peacemaker President” ending the “forever wars”, and threatens China, the European Union, Gaza, Iran, Venezuela, Colombia, among others. But the reality, say the many critics, is that decline is real. In the words of Adam Tooze: “Hegemonic decline [of the US] is a done deal. It’s over” (Wallace-Wells ).

The argument of US hegemonic decline—and the rise of geopolitical competitors—rests partly on evidence of US relative economic decline as measured by national accounts (Desai ; Hickel and Sullivan ; Mearsheimer ; Sachs ). We argue that US economic decline is greatly exaggerated, and many commentators fail to see this because they overlook how globalization has changed the way we should conceptualize national economic power (Wade ). Once we understand how American power has globalized (Starrs , ), the virtually insurmountable nature of US hegemony in global capitalism becomes clear, for the foreseeable future. This is partly because of—not despite—China’s capitalist rise and integration into this system.

In the following, we first highlight persistent global inequality despite decades of global South growth. We then argue that this largely stems from the power of US TNCs generally and particularly in advanced technology (such as semiconductors) which the US state can weaponize; also from the dollar’s global role, the structure of global finance, and dominant US position in the most important international organizations. These sinews are interwoven in global capitalism itself, privileging and reinforcing US power such that challenging one would necessitate challenging others.

### America’s average income relative to the global South

The standard metric used to substantiate the claim of US economic decline is share of global GDP. US share of global GDP at market exchange rates fell from 30.3% in 2000 to 26% in 2024 (recovering from a postwar low of 21% in 2011). Conversely, China’s share of world GDP soared from 3.6% in 2000 to 17% in 2024 (down from 18% in 2021).

Using this common GDP metric, we have to make at least two qualifications to the standard story of US decline. First, while the US share has fallen by several percentage points, the US continues to have by far the biggest GDP in the world, with only 4.2% of the population. China has 18% of the population, roughly equal to its share of world GDP. At market exchange rates China’s per capita GDP is only about 20% of the US’s.

Second, the long-established two-bloc structure of the world economy – the West and the non-West – has _strengthened_ over the past several decades (with one crucial exception: China), despite the claims that the prevailing world order of neoliberal “globalization” generates income catch-up (hence is legitimate). In particular, the three regions of Latin America and the Caribbean, Middle East and Central Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa have _lower_ average incomes relative to the US in 2023 than in 1980 (per capita GDP at purchasing power parity exchange rates).

*   Latin America’s and the Caribbean’s average income fell from 38% to 25% of the US’s by 2023;

*   Middle East and Central Asia’s fell from 39% to 19%;

*   Sub-Saharan Africa’s fell from 10% to 6%.

*   Emerging and developing Asia without China’s rose slightly after 1980 to reach almost 15% by 2023;

*   China’s rose from a little above 2% in 1980 to almost 29% of the US’s by 2023;

*   South Korea is the standout catch-up economy, whose GDP per capita relative to the US’s rose from 17% in 1980 to almost 70% in 2023 (UNCTAD 2024, fig. III.2).

These trends are fundamentally satisfactory for the US and the wider West. The bloc of western states plus Japan has a strong interest in protecting the international capitalist economic system which keeps these states on top, protecting their ability to “make” the rules that others must “take”. They have a built-in incentive to justify their superior position in line with what could be called a “law” of modern-era power hierarchies: elites legitimize their success in terms of universalistic and meritocratic qualities, like initiative, hard work, commitment to the scientific method; and legitimize others’ lower rank in terms of their failure to match these qualities, their excessive dedication to identity politics, corruption, leisure (and white populations have long used the non-universalistic criterion of skin color to claim superiority).

To sustain this narrative, attention must be diverted from the sinews of power by which the US and western allies remain on top. We now examine several of these.

### America abroad: TNCs

One of many problems with the GDP metric is that it includes only the value of goods and services produced within a given state’s boundaries. It overlooks that some states – the US above all – are able to extract vast resources from the territory of other states; and that other territories and their people do not have ownership of a significant portion of their country’s GDP. That includes China.

A dense network of transnational corporations (TNCs) is one of the US’s channels for resource extraction. The network has been the biggest in the world since the 1960s (when international corporate rankings emerged), and in some sectors relatively even bigger today. These TNCs establish production organizations in other countries—in high-income countries in Europe and the Gulf, and lower income countries like China and Vietnam—where their operations contribute to the hosts’ GDP. But the profits accrue mostly to the TNC owners outside the countries of production.

For example, China is the world’s biggest assembler and exporter of iPhones. The primary assembler, Foxconn, a Taiwanese firm, is China’s largest private employer (over one million workers), and Zhengzhou is dubbed “iPhone City”. But Apple, not a Chinese firm, owns the iPhone and receives most of the profits from sales. For the iPhone 15 Pro Max released in 2023, Apple retails it for a minimum of $1,199, cost of production is $523, of which less than $13 is paid to outsourced firms in China for assembly and components.[Footnote 1](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#) Apple can invest a large share of the profits in innovation and marketing to keep ahead of Chinese rivals, maintaining its dominant profit-share.

The larger point is that a big share of China’s hi-tech exports are produced by “foreign-invested enterprises” (including joint-ventures), and only a relatively small share by state- and privately-owned Chinese enterprises. China’s Customs Statistical Yearbook gives figures for exports of goods assembled in China with advanced imported components, the category called “process with imported materials exports”. This includes iPhones and virtually all advanced tech exports (which totaled more than $800 billion in 2022, 22% of total exports). From 2004-2021 80% or more of this major category of exports came from foreign-invested enterprises; by 2024-2025 it fell to 68%.

China is commonly called “the workshop of the world”. It might more sharply be called “the foreign-owned workshop of the world". China’s rise has benefited US TNCs by keeping their production costs lower while also being an increasingly important consumer market as China’s middle class has expanded. In 2023 for the first time, China surpassed the United States as the biggest market for Apple’s iPhones.

### America abroad: share of global profits

The next step in substantiating the US’s continuing economic dominance is to identify the extent to which global corporate profits accrue to US TNCs as distinct from those of other countries. _Forbes Global 2000_ gives profit data for the 2,000 biggest TNCs by sector since 2003. In 25 broad sectors, US TNCs accrue the biggest share of profits in 19 of the 25 (76%) as of 2025.[Footnote 2](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#) China leads in four (banking; construction; forestry, metals and mining; telecommunications), Japan leads in the remaining two.

China’s rise over the past two decades (and western Europe’s and Japan’s relative decline) is certainly impressive, but it must be put in this context, where China has reached the biggest share of global profits (of the 2,000 TNCs in the _Forbes Global 2000_) in four sectors out of 25, US TNCs in 19. In 2025 the US dominates in advanced technology profit-shares such as 63% in aerospace and defense, 85% in computer hardware and software (despite China being the world’s largest PC-maker and having the most software developers), 46% in electronics (despite China being the world’s largest exporter since 2004), 45% in heavy machinery, 64% in pharmaceuticals. National accounts cannot account for this dominance in the age of globalization.

### America abroad: near-monopolies in crucial technologies

We have seen that the US has the biggest network of TNCs, and this network reaps the biggest share of global profits in three quarters of the 25 major sectors of the world economy. The next point is that US firms have near-monopolies in many crucial technologies, from smartphone operating systems to semiconductor design software.

Huawei was China’s most advanced global competitor in the 2010s. Its smartphone was the biggest global brand in the second quarter of 2020. But when the US Department of Commerce placed Huawei on the “Entity List” in May 2019, TNCs (both US and non-US using US-owned technology) were legally prevented from doing business with it, it could not update its Google Android operating system or access the most advanced semiconductors from Taiwan’s TSMC. Its global smartphone market share collapsed from 20% in the second quarter of 2020 to virtually zero outside of China in 2021-2024.

While Apple sells slightly fewer smartphones in China than Huawei in 2025 (16.4% vs 16.2%), it dominates with by far the most profit because of its margins. In 2024, Apple reaped 72% of profits in the global smartphone market while Samsung Electronics captured 24%, with less than 4% for all Chinese smartphone firms combined (despite Vivo, Huawei, Xiaomi, OPPO, and Honor with a combined market-share by units in China of 77%).[Footnote 3](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)

In this Huawei smartphone case, the US state acted in May 2019 during the first Trump administration. The US state acted even more strongly from October 2022 onwards during the Biden administration to put stringent export controls on advanced semiconductors to China and on the equipment to make them. It banned US TNCs and even US citizens and green card holders from doing business or research in the advanced semiconductor industry in China. It exerted extra-territorial pressure on _non-US_ firms at the pinnacle of the global chip industry, and on their governments, not to supply advanced chips or equipment to China, a point we return to shortly.

The release of China’s chatbot DeepSeek R1 in January 2025 – which exceeded OpenAI’s most advanced model on several benchmarks at what it claimed was 10% of the cost,[Footnote 4](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#) using less sophisticated (not banned) chips from US company Nvidia – shows a China-based capacity to innovate within these US-led constraints. On the other hand, US companies like Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft have no constraints on their access to the world’s most advanced chips, an advantage for the marathon: there is 39.7 million petaflops of AI compute power in the US versus 400,000 in China (Slabaugh and Starrs ).

In chips and several other advanced technologies, the US state uses the global dominance of US TNCs as the lever to exert power over other states and to help the TNCs channel profits back home.

This is a relatively new power that arose because of the manner in which China integrated into global capitalism. The US did not possess it as it attempted to contain Soviet technological advance from the 1950s, when the Soviets advanced faster than the US in major military technologies like intercontinental ballistic missiles, rockets and satellites for two decades, using indigenously developed advanced technology. At that time the Soviet Union was barely economically integrated into global capitalism and the US state did not have such levers to deprive it of critical technologies. The US state now does have this power with respect to China today—which reflects that China invited foreign capital post-1978 to help develop its technology, and China has not managed to unshackle its techno-dependency on the West to this day.

The US also could not cut off Japan’s access to advanced technology when Japanese semiconductor firms surpassed the US in the mid-1980s. While not as protectionist as the Soviet Union, Japan was still able to develop its technology indigenously, so Reagan had to employ traditional tools such as slapping 100% tariffs on Japanese chips and a $500 million industrial policy involving Sematech, a public-private partnership between the US state and 14 US chip firms. The strategy worked, as US firms regained their 50% world market-share by the mid-1990s where it has remained ever since.

But US tools are qualitatively different now against China because China depends on key US-owned technologies (including advanced chip design software and equipment).

In short, the rise of China, heavily integrated into global capitalism, has _strengthened_ the US state’s structural power over it, as China is _more dependent_ on supply chains controlled by the US. This is not to deny that China has also increased its economic power in the 21 st century (especially vis-à-vis East Asia and Western Europe), but to stress that China’s dependent integration into a US-centered global capitalism makes it more vulnerable to US techno-containment, as we shall see.

Patent revenue data reinforce the point. By 2022 China was filing more patents than the rest of the world combined. But this includes patents filed only in China, many of which are spurious. A better measure of technological innovation than patent filings is a country’s balance of payments in intellectual property licensing, which reflects _patent commercialization_. Here the US is the world winner, by far (authors’ calculations from World Bank ). Its surplus in intellectual property licensing fees rose steadily from $24 billion in 1997 to $115 billion in 2024 (current nominal value). Over the same period China went from a deficit of $488 million to a deficit of $36 billion. The 2024 deficit of $36 billion is a large increase from the deficit of $20 billion in 2013—which is striking because China has spent tens of billions of dollars in industrial policy since 2014 aimed at reversing this techno-dependency. China is paying out far more for access to foreign technologies than it is receiving for foreigners’ access to its own patented technologies, indicating vastly lower demand for China’s innovation.[Footnote 5](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)

### America abroad: the US-centered advanced semiconductor ecosystem

Semiconductors are the single-most foundational advanced technology of the 21st century because they drive all electronics and software. US-based TNCs dominate the global semiconductor value chain. A summary measure (Starrs , 138-141) comes from the revenues of the 50 biggest TNCs operating in the six main segments of the chain, based in nine countries. The segments extend from electronic design automation (EDA) software to fabless design, from foundries and assembly, packaging, testing (APT) to materials and semiconductor manufacturing equipment (SME).

US-based TNCs have the biggest share of revenues in three of these segments – the three most intellectual- property-intensive (EDA software, fabless design, SME); and second biggest in two (foundries, materials). In the separate category of “integrated device manufacturers” which both design and manufacture their chips, US TNCs (_eg_ Intel) also have the biggest share.

Altogether, US-based TNCs accrue 45% of the total revenue of these 50 semiconductor TNCs, with South Korean and Taiwanese firms second (18%) and third (17%). South Korea and Taiwan are close US allies. China ties with Switzerland for 7 th and 8 th place (2%).

China is heavily dependent on chip imports. Its share of the world’s chip imports surged to 40% by 2007, and has remained above 40% since 2015. This despite China’s huge industrial policies worth tens of billions of dollars unleashed in 2014 (China Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund) and 2015 (Made in China 2025) specifically to reduce foreign techno-dependence.

We noted earlier that the US state under the Biden administration imposed stringent export controls on the sale of advanced semiconductors to China, and on the equipment to make them. It banned US TNCs and even US citizens and green card holders from doing business or research in the advanced semiconductor industry in China. In effect, Biden applied export controls not just on individual Chinese firms but on the entire Chinese chip industry. This is a measure of US power over China.

Biden also exerted extra-territorial pressure on _non-US_ firms at the pinnacle of the global chip industry, and on their governments, not to supply advanced chips or equipment to China.

The US state’s main instrument of control over US and non-US firms starts with the Entity List, a list of firms, individuals, research labs and some states (_eg_ North Korea) to which no US entity can export without a license, with a presumption of denial. The Export Control Reform Act of August 2018 extended this to _non-US firms_, banned from exporting goods containing US-derived technology. The earthquake occurred in May 2019 when the Trump administration placed China’s most internationally successful tech firm, Huawei, on the Entity List. In December 2020, Trump placed China’s leading foundry on the Entity List, SMIC (Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation). In December 2022 Biden placed China’s leading memory chip maker, YMTC, on the Entity List.

The bottom line is that from 2018 to March 2025 the US state raised the number of Chinese targets on the Entity List from 218 to 1,110 (Starrs _et al_ 2025 [Citation forthcoming](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)).

Biden’s CHIPS and Science Act, August 2022, included large subsidies for production of chips within the US, whether domestic or foreign companies. The subsidies carried conditions, one of which was that any recipient would have to commit to not invest in producing sophisticated chips in China for a decade (sophisticated was defined as 16 nanometers or smaller). The Act further banned US citizens and permanent residents from working in China on sophisticated chips, including Taiwanese or Chinese dual-citizens, such as SMIC founder Richard Chang and the CEO of memory chip maker YMTC Simon Yang, both of whom resigned from their powerful positions in China’s chip industry (McMorrow _et al_)!

As though this was not enough, Trump clarified in May 2025 that anyone _in the world_ using Huawei chips would breach US export controls, since they involved US-owned technology.

Armed with these and other weapons, the US state pressured three pinnacle firms in the global chip supply chain not to supply China, _all of them based outside the US._ Namely,

*   ASML, the Dutch company with a 100% world monopoly on the production of the equipment for the most advanced chips (EUVs, extreme ultraviolet photolithography).

*   Tokyo Electron (TEL), the Japanese company with 100% world monopoly on the crucial coater/developer piece of equipment needed in EUVs.

*   TSMC, the Taiwanese company with a virtual monopoly on the mass production of advanced chips (the only company that mass produces 3-4 nanometer chips, until Intel started to ramp up to commercial-scale production of 1.8 nanometers in late 2025 thanks to CHIPS Act subsidies).

The US state was able to exert this extra-territorial leverage because even these powerful non-US TNCs depend on US-owned “chokepoint” technology, through which the US can weaponize their “techno-dependency” to make them comply with the blockade against China in advanced chips and equipment.

For example, the Dutch state granted a license for ASML to sell its EUV photolithography machines to China’s leading chip maker SMIC, with planned delivery in 2019. The Trump administration put the Dutch government under “heavy political pressure”. Dutch Trade Minister Schreinemacher admitted that the US had been “pressuring us for two years”, and the largest Dutch tech association urged that “The Netherlands would be helped by more unified and powerful action by the European Union” (Sterling ). The Dutch government gave way and allowed the license to expire without renewal before ASML could ship them. In 2026 there are still no EUV machines in China.

The key instrument of this “pressure” is US export controls on non-US firms using US-owned technology, a significant part of which was developed with US government funding (in the case of photolithography machines, since a 1967 US Airforce contract). The US invoked this Foreign Direct Products Rule against TSMC in 2020 (_eg_ US ownership of the EDA software used to design Taiwanese semiconductors), cutting off blacklisted Chinese firms’ access to the most advanced chips. And once the US ensured that ASML could not sell its EUV equipment to China there was no point in Japan/TEL selling related equipment to China.

Both the Dutch and Japanese governments circumvented their own laws, their commitment to “free trade”, and the powerful material interests of their leading firms, to comply with the “techno-nationalist” objectives of the hegemon (Starrs and Germann ). Moreover, the US techno-nationalist state has also overridden the powerful profit interest of Silicon Valley and Wall Street, with their strong incentives to continue selling into the biggest and fastest growing market for semiconductors in the world.

All three of these peak non-US firms in the semiconductor supply chain depend on US-owned and US-made technology, either directly (ASML) or indirectly (TEL). We should note the other side—they also all depend heavily on US customers. For example, TSMC’s biggest customer is Apple, followed by Nvidia. And US-based manufacturing facilities are by far the biggest source of foreign advanced technology for those non-US firms.[Footnote 6](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)

The compliance of non-US chip firms to US state export controls is reinforced by Wall Street’s equity ownership of these firms. For example, in June 2024[Footnote 7](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)

*   TSMC: Wall Street owns 43% of the shares, domestic Taiwan equity owners only 9%, the remainder “other foreign”;

*   ASML: Wall Street owns 51%, Dutch equity owners 4%;

*   TEL: Wall Street owns 24%, Japanese equity owners 56%;

*   Samsung Electronics has the biggest revenues from chip production in the world; for the whole company (including smartphones, laptops, _etc_) Wall Street owns 32%, Korean shareholders 40%.

*   The top 10 non-US chip firms by revenue in 2024 (including the ones just listed): Wall Street holds an average of 35% of their equity, domestic owners, 30%.

This, then, is yet another measure of US structural power in the world system, as Wall Street represents by far the biggest source of foreign funding for the vast majority of non-US firms. The average US ownership of the top 50 Chinese firms in 2021 (the great bulk of which are state-owned enterprises) was 9.7% compared to 64% Chinese ownership, ranging up to 13% US ownership of Bank of China and 29% of Alibaba (authors’ calculations from _Bloomberg Professional_ and corporate annual reports). In sharp contrast, the top 50 US TNCs are owned in large majority by American owners—82% versus 5.2% for the largest foreign owner (UK).

However, China’s leading chip maker, SMIC, has made significant progress even without the sophisticated EUV machines, allowing Huawei to release the Mate 60 Pro smartphone using SMIC’s homegrown 7 nanometer chips in August 2023. But since then, SMIC has been stuck at the 7 nm level, while Apple’s 2024 iPhone uses 3 nm chips from TSMC. Huawei was releasing 4 nm chip phones in 2022 and 7 nm in 2019 when they had access to Samsung Electronics and TSMC, yet Huawei is still releasing products in 2025 with 7 nm chips. US techno-containment is working, so far (Wade [Citation 2025a](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)).

## America abroad: the dollar weapon

The US dollar is a vital sinew of American power; and its global dominance is reinforced by the global dominance of Wall Street in financial services (Wade ), with 68% _Forbes Global 2000_ profit-share in 2025.

The dollar as of 2025 is involved on one side of 88-89% of all foreign exchange transactions, only slightly lower than in 1989, testimony to the dollar’s resilience. It accounts for 58% of global foreign exchange reserves; and 64% of world debt is denominated in dollars.

The dollar’s dominance rests on the US’s structural trade deficit, amounting to 3% to 4% of GDP in recent decades, which ensures an ample supply of dollar liquidity into the world economy. That generates huge incumbency advantages—institutions which give the dollar system economies of scale and network externalities, such that the more users the more people need to use it. The institutions include Wall Street and US Big Tech (Google, Facebook, Apple, Amazon, Microsoft), which dominate the world system and have no incentives to de-dollarize. This means the US has the world’s deepest, most liquid, most open financial markets, with no replacement on the horizon.

Dollar dominance in turn allows the US to sustain large current account deficits—import much more than it earns from exports, thereby “artificially” boosting consumption for a large subset of Americans. Also, it lowers US corporations’ cost of foreign direct investment (FDI), fueling their expansion around the world. And it allows the US to easily finance its military activities around the world, further reinforcing US power (Lambert and Plihon ).

Established half a century ago, SWIFT (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication) is the crucial nerve center of the global financial network, the message system through which all major banks transfer all major currencies. It carries over five billion financial messages a year. It does not actually move money; it simply tells one bank to debit an account and credit another. A Shanghai bank wanting to send funds to a Sydney bank must go through SWIFT.

The US does not formally control SWIFT—it is a cooperative owned by major central banks and overseen by the National Bank of Belgium. In practice the US is able to get SWIFT to do what it wants, by and large.

Kicking a country off SWIFT constitutes a severe national blow. In 2012 the US (the European Union went along) kicked Iran off SWIFT, which meant it could not receive hard currencies for its oil exports. The blow was severe enough, coupled with a change in government plus a change in US policy on nuclear enrichment, for Iran to come to the bargaining table in 2015 and agree with the US to restrain its nuclear program (Gibson ). The US kicked Russia off SWIFT after its early 2022 invasion of Ukraine; or to be more exact, kicked 10 of the largest Russian financial institutions off SWIFT. While this and other sanctions did not crash the Russian economy because its main exports can be redirected to China in RMB, it became far more dependent on China and therefore vulnerable.

Over the past decade the US government has increasingly used its control over the dollar—whether directly or via SWIFT—to sanction countries and private entities and individuals that it deems hostile. Russia’s president Putin lamented in 2018, “We aren’t aiming to ditch the dollar. The dollar is ditching us” (Andrianova and Biryukov ). A measure of China’s concern over US retaliation is that Chinese state-owned banks (as well as the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank) have cut Russian banks off from dollar financing, respecting US sanctions.

Most banks globally have no choice but to comply with US sanctions, because they cannot afford to let go of correspondent banking relationships with US banks (and Wall Street more generally). If banks’ transactions with US enemies are in dollars they will pass through a US-based clearing house, making them subject to US laws.

We should also not omit the dollar system’s geopolitical underpinnings. The US has at least 700 overseas military bases spread over 80 countries, and a military budget bigger than the next 10 countries combined. This is especially relevant to the case of Middle East oil and its pricing in dollars. China does not (yet) have the military capacity to persuade US allies in the Gulf to switch out of dollars. For example, Iraq, the region’s second-largest oil producer, had to abandon its attempt to partially trade in China’s RMB in July 2024 when the US Federal Reserve ordered the Iraqi Central Bank to suspend RMB settlement.[Footnote 8](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)

## America abroad: wealth receipts from the global South

The West has operated since the 1990s with a “hyper-globalist” model of progress, in which financial capital is free to move in and out across (most) national borders. Economic growth in this financialized era depends increasingly on rising levels of _debt_—on asset bubbles at home or, for Germany, Japan and China, asset bubbles and current account deficits elsewhere, especially the US.

The owners of financial capital seek yield in high-risk, high-return assets around the world. This has produced the combination of record levels of northern stock markets and global debt relative to GDP. US government debt rose from about a third of GDP in 2000 to around 100% in 2024.

Western agents, including the IMF and World Bank, have pressed emerging economies to integrate fully into the global financial system, lifting restrictions on, first, the inflow and outflow of finance and second, the establishment of foreign financial corporations in their territory (Wade , ). This is why so many non-US firms have such high Wall Street ownership now, including in “emerging economies” (Starrs ).

As emerging economies integrate into the global financial system they become vulnerable to changes in western (especially US) interest rates, exchange rates, asset prices, which generate swings in _capital inflows and outflows_ and also swings in the _value_ of those countries’ stocks of international assets and liabilities.

Not only are they vulnerable to western instabilities (remember that the US central bank’s mandate requires it to take no consideration of the effects of its domestically-oriented decisions on the rest of the world), they also transfer huge amounts of wealth to the core countries.

Some of the wealth transfers come from equities, in the form of capital (or wealth) losses and yield losses (payments on liabilities minus income from assets). The South’s capital losses and yield differentials on equities go mainly to the private sector in the West, boosting income inequality in the West. In 2023, North America owned an astounding 46% of global financial assets (totaling $275.2 trillion), while the Asia-Pacific (excluding Japan) owned 22%, western Europe 19%, Japan 5.6%, and Latin America and the Middle East & Africa owned 2.9% each.[Footnote 9](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)

As for debt, a large part of the transfers go to international lenders (banks and bond holders) in the West. Another large part goes to Northern governments, mainly the US, through purchases of US Treasuries as foreign exchange reserves, which in effect gives a subsidy to the US government.

The upshot is that 16 emerging economies over 2000-2018 (including China, India and Brazil) transferred around 2.2% of their combined GDP per year on average, almost all to advanced western countries, especially US, Germany, UK, and Japan (UNCTAD , chapter 5). The combination of low-yielding assets in their reserves (notably US Treasury bills) and high-yielding liabilities generates what looks to be “protection money” paid to the core—the source of the shocks. It is not so far from the Mafia model, where the Mafia boss tells the shop owner, “You pay me, I’ll protect you—from me”. It is yet another mechanism for maintaining the hierarchical core-periphery structure of the world economy. Ironically in the case of China investing large proportions of its foreign exchange surpluses in US Treasury bills (directly and indirectly via asset managers in Belgium and Luxembourg), China is helping the US finance its cold war against China.

## America abroad: dominant position in the legacy multilateral economic organizations

The US remains the biggest shareholder in the legacy multilateral economic organizations created in the twentieth century, including the IMF, World Bank, Interamerican Development Bank, Asian Development Bank (alongside Japan), and European Bank for Reconstruction and Development; also the biggest non-regional shareholder in the African Development Bank. Recent, painfully negotiated capital increases at several multilateral development banks, and IMF quota increases, have left formal power in the hands of the US and other legacy western states. The US continues to have a veto over major decisions at the IMF, World Bank, and Interamerican Development Bank. Veto power protects veto power (Vestergaard and Wade , Forthcoming; Wade , [Citation 2025b](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#); Wade and Vestergaard ).

An inflection point arrived in 2015, when China led the creation of a new regional development bank, the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank based in Beijing, and India with Brazil and China led the creation of the world’s first _global_ development bank since the World Bank, the New Development Bank based in Shanghai. The US is a member of neither. Both have cooperated more than challenged the legacy organizations – indeed, cooperation is emphasized in their Articles of Agreement (Batista ). Since 2020, Chinese international lending practices (for example, in the Belt Road Initiative) have converged with the West’s, with China being increasingly concerned with debt repayment (Hameiri and Jones ).

## America and global capitalism interwoven

When forecasting the “end of global capitalism” by “secular stagnation” or some other economic crisis, too many theorize from high up in the clouds (Freeman ; Li ; Patnaik ). Their economic determinism—driven by the cyclical rise, fall, and/or stagnation of relative national accounts—is stripped of the agency of those that shape and reshape global capitalism back on Earth, states and TNCs, preventing self-implosion from internal contradictions. Not even two world wars, the Great Depression, and the international expansion of various socialist revolutions from 1917 to the 1970s could derail capitalism—it has only since deepened and widened. After Wall Street crashed in 2008, its global profit-share increased from 47% in 2007 to 66% by 2013, 68% in 2025. Hence if there is secular stagnation (or some other “crisis”), this is not necessarily a crisis for either the hegemon or global capitalism (let alone a sign of its imminent demise). Unlike in the natural world, there are no “laws of economics” divorced from human action; capitalism is socially constructed and can be socially deconstructed and reconstructed.

The most powerful actors have the greatest capacity to do this and by far the most powerful in history is the US state. America “made global capitalism” in the post-1945 period (Panitch and Gindin ), and part of its success to this day is constructing a global system in which the rise of at least a few powerful non-western states within this system makes it and America’s position more enduring. This is because their capitalists (of which there are more billionaires and millionaires in China than any other except the United States) benefit from capitalism and therefore have a stake in protecting and promoting it, even if the hegemon benefits the most. The US prioritizes its own systemic hegemony and does not accept any _potential_ contenders, which is why it acted against Japan in the 1980s and China post-2018. The US is harsher on the latter because unlike Japan, China remains geopolitically independent. The difference from the 1980s is that the US power toolbox has expanded precisely because of how China has risen, by integrating into US-centered global value chains—rendering China vulnerable to US weaponization (Farrell and Newman ; Starrs ; Starrs _et al_ forthcoming)

This is not to deny China’s great success story. Recall that its average income (in PPP terms) rose from slightly above 2% of the US’s in 1980 to almost 29% by 2023, with a giant demographic mass four times the US’s. By 2023 its share of world manufacturing was 29%, more than the US, Japan, and Germany combined (27%).[Footnote 10](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#) Chinese infrastructure puts the United States and Europe to shame. China dominates the global production and installation of solar panels and wind turbines, as well as coal production. In 2020 it graduated four times as many STEM students than the US and deploys more industrial robots than the rest of the world combined. Over the 2000s China has come to dominate the global supply of rare earth minerals and magnets made with them, which are needed for everything from semiconductors and electric vehicles to smartphones, missiles and fighter jets. In late 2025 China shocked foreign governments and businesses by announcing rules limiting the flow of these minerals and magnets, following the model the US uses for weaponizing control of semiconductors and the tools to make them.

All this and much more is very impressive, but it would only be a threat to US hegemony if China sought to break free from the sinews of US structural power and carve out its own alternative for others to follow. This would entail, at minimum, dethroning the dollar with an alternative global transactions currency. Another country or a grouping like BRICS+ would have to run balance of payments deficits so their currency can spread globally, which would require limited or no capital controls. Since the 2015 Shanghai market crash, China has only strengthened its capital controls, and again with its post-2020 crackdown on fintech and others. The party-state prioritizes its own political stability over capitalist profit and free cross-border movement and will likely maintain strict capital controls as long as this is the case. This means the RMB cannot replace the dollar.

Moreover, China would have to develop military power projection overseas to defend against US attack, including in the Persian Gulf, where China has no forward capacity to challenge the more than 50,000 US troops stationed there. It does not even have the capacity to prevent the US Navy from sailing through what it considers to be its exclusive economic zone in the South China Sea. For the BRICS+ to defend against US attack, they would have to overcome their differences to ally in mutual defense (China and India still disagree on their borders, and traditionally support non-alignment). That China has respected US-led military export bans to Russia in its post-2022 NATO proxy war is a measure of China’s concern over facing further US sanctions (whereas Iran and North Korea are the only nations that have militarily supported Russia).

For the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) to transition from the petrodollar to the petroyuan, they would have to sever their decades-long security dependence upon the United States. They would only do this if China could defend them against US, Israeli, and Iranian attack – all engaging in Gulf War III at the time of writing. After over a month of escalating war crimes and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, it is remarkable how little role China is playing in mediating the conflict. China abstained in the passing of United Nations Security Council Resolution 2817 on 11 March 2026, which condemned Iran’s retaliatory strikes on the GCC without mentioning the prior Israeli-US international crime of aggression from 28 February. It is unclear if China will even have any significant role in postwar infrastructure reconstruction, since China also abstained in the November 2025 Security Council Resolution 2803 approving Trump’s "Board of Peace" for the "redevelopment of Gaza". By February 2026, the GCC pledged over $4 billion to Trump’s Board, whose mission expanded across the region, and post-Gulf War III most will likely double-down on their security relationships with the United States.

China challenging the United States would amount to a fundamental re-ordering of post-1945 global capitalism, and even if China had the capacity (we have shown that it does not), the United States would not sit idly by. Great powers, let alone hegemons, do not react peacefully to existential threats, and the US is not shy about waging wars, directly and indirectly, to pursue its interests.[Footnote 11](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#) This is most obvious since 1945 in West Asia, where US control of oil grants substantial control of the global political economy (Hanieh ). This control has only increased since 9/11, as the US has, covertly and overtly, toppled the governments of Iraq, Libya, Syria (reducing Russian regional influence), and severely weakened Hezbollah, Palestine, and Yemen in conjunction with its key ally Israel. All of which has weakened the last remaining significant independent actor in the region, Iran—with China playing no counter-balancing role in this onslaught. The sinews of global capitalism and US hegemony will continue to be interwoven as they have tightened since 1945; “unweaving” one cannot be done without “unweaving” the other.

This is not to argue that there can be no end to global capitalism. It _is_ to argue that its demise will not come at the hands of those who benefit from the system, namely, capitalists of the world (notably those based in the US, Japan, Europe, and especially China, capitalism’s primary beneficiary in the twenty-first century[Footnote 12](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#)). At the end of the day, capitalists of the world would rather join the post-1945 global capitalist order than fundamentally challenge the hegemon, even when the hegemon demands higher costs of entry, as in Trump’s global tariff war. The alternative—decoupling from the United States and building a new semi-global system in the face of overwhelming US resistance—would be vastly more costly than maneuvering within the system to pursue one’s interests. This accounts for the extraordinary persistence of US structural power (with its global concentration of corporate power and wealth) across the past eight decades despite the reconstruction and revival of western Europe and Japan, the rise of the East Asian newly industrializing countries, the challenge of socialism and the Nonaligned Movement, OPEC, more recently the rise of BRICS+ and most of all China. Only anti-capitalist forces could threaten global capitalism, and since none are currently on the horizon,[Footnote 13](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2329194X.2026.2675224#) there is a greater chance of ecocide and interplanetary colonization than the end of capitalism this century.
