# On the Duty of Civil Disobedience

**Author**: Henry David Thoreau (1817–1862)
**Published**: 1849
**Original title**: Resistance to Civil Government
**Source**: [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71/71-h/71-h.htm)

---

## Overview

This is a seminal essay in American political philosophy in which Thoreau argues that individuals have a moral duty to resist unjust government through nonviolent civil disobedience. Written in response to the Mexican-American War and the institution of slavery, the essay has influenced generations of political activists, including Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

## Core Argument

Thoreau's central thesis is that government is fundamentally an **expedient**—a practical tool, not a moral authority. When government becomes inexpedient or actively harmful, citizens have both the right and the duty to resist it. The essay challenges the notion that majority rule equates to justice and argues that individual conscience must supersede legal obligation when the two conflict.

## Key Themes

### 1. Government as Expedient
Government is "at best but an expedient" and should be viewed with the same suspicion as standing armies. The character of the people, not the machinery of government, drives progress.

### 2. Majority Rule vs. Conscience
Majority rule persists not because majorities are right, but because they are physically strongest. A government based on majority opinion "cannot be based on justice." The only true obligation is "to do at any time what I think right."

### 3. Three Modes of Service
Thoreau distinguishes three ways of serving the state:
- **Body servants**: Physical labor without moral judgment (soldiers, jailers)
- **Head servants**: Intellectual service without moral distinction (politicians, lawyers)
- **Conscience servants**: Moral resistance to the state (heroes, reformers, martyrs)

### 4. Direct Action
Thoreau's personal act of resistance—refusing to pay the poll tax for six years and spending one night in jail (1846)—demonstrates that individual action can be a powerful form of protest. "If a thousand men were not to pay their tax-bills this year, that would not be a violent and bloody measure."

### 5. The Folly of Voting
Voting is "a sort of gaming, like chequers or backgammon, with a slight moral tinge to it." Even voting for the right is doing nothing for it. Only those who assert their own freedom through action can hasten justice.

### 6. Breaking Unjust Laws
"If [the injustice] is of such a nature that it requires you to be the agent of injustice to another, then, I say, break the law. Let your life be a counter friction to stop the machine."

### 7. The True Place for a Just Man
"Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison." This is "the only house in a slave-state in which a free man can abide with honor."

### 8. Property and Virtue
"Absolutely speaking, the more money, the less virtue." The rich are "always sold to the institution which makes him rich."

## Historical Context

- **Mexican-American War (1846–1848)**: Thoreau opposed the war as an unjust expansion of slavery
- **Slavery**: A sixth of the U.S. population were slaves at the time of writing
- **Tax Resistance**: Thoreau refused the poll tax from 1842–1848, spending one night in jail in July 1846
- **Influence**: Gandhi cited this essay as a major influence on his philosophy of satyagraha; Martin Luther King Jr. called it his "first intellectual contact with the theory of nonviolent resistance"

## Significance

This essay is one of the foundational texts of modern civil disobedience theory. It established the principle that individuals have a higher moral obligation to justice than to law, and that passive resistance can be a powerful tool for social change. The essay's influence extends far beyond American politics, shaping resistance movements worldwide.
