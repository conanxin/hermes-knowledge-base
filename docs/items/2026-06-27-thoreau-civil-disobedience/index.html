On the Duty of Civil Disobedience

Author: Henry David Thoreau (1817–1862) Published: 1849 Original title: Resistance to Civil Government Source: Project Gutenberg

Overview

This is a seminal essay in American political philosophy in which Thoreau argues that individuals have a moral duty to resist unjust government through nonviolent civil disobedience. Written in response to the Mexican-American War and the institution of slavery, the essay has influenced generations of political activists, including Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

Core Argument

Thoreau's central thesis is that government is fundamentally an expedient—a practical tool, not a moral authority. When government becomes inexpedient or actively harmful, citizens have both the right and the duty to resist it. The essay challenges the notion that majority rule equates to justice and argues that individual conscience must supersede legal obligation when the two conflict.

Key Themes

1. Government as Expedient

Government is "at best but an expedient" and should be viewed with the same suspicion as standing armies. The character of the people, not the machinery of government, drives progress.

2. Majority Rule vs. Conscience

Majority rule persists not because majorities are right, but because they are physically strongest. A government based on majority opinion "cannot be based on justice." The only true obligation is "to do at any time what I think right."

3. Three Modes of Service

Thoreau distinguishes three ways of serving the state:

  • Body servants: Physical labor without moral judgment (soldiers, jailers)
  • Head servants: Intellectual service without moral distinction (politicians, lawyers)
  • Conscience servants: Moral resistance to the state (heroes, reformers, martyrs)

4. Direct Action

Thoreau's personal act of resistance—refusing to pay the poll tax for six years and spending one night in jail (1846)—demonstrates that individual action can be a powerful form of protest. "If a thousand men were not to pay their tax-bills this year, that would not be a violent and bloody measure."

5. The Folly of Voting

Voting is "a sort of gaming, like chequers or backgammon, with a slight moral tinge to it." Even voting for the right is doing nothing for it. Only those who assert their own freedom through action can hasten justice.

6. Breaking Unjust Laws

"If [the injustice] is of such a nature that it requires you to be the agent of injustice to another, then, I say, break the law. Let your life be a counter friction to stop the machine."

7. The True Place for a Just Man

"Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison." This is "the only house in a slave-state in which a free man can abide with honor."

8. Property and Virtue

"Absolutely speaking, the more money, the less virtue." The rich are "always sold to the institution which makes him rich."

Historical Context

  • Mexican-American War (1846–1848): Thoreau opposed the war as an unjust expansion of slavery
  • Slavery: A sixth of the U.S. population were slaves at the time of writing
  • Tax Resistance: Thoreau refused the poll tax from 1842–1848, spending one night in jail in July 1846
  • Influence: Gandhi cited this essay as a major influence on his philosophy of satyagraha; Martin Luther King Jr. called it his "first intellectual contact with the theory of nonviolent resistance"

Significance

This essay is one of the foundational texts of modern civil disobedience theory. It established the principle that individuals have a higher moral obligation to justice than to law, and that passive resistance can be a powerful tool for social change. The essay's influence extends far beyond American politics, shaping resistance movements worldwide.

论公民不服从

原标题：对政府的抵抗（Resistance to Civil Government）

核心论点

政府本质上是一种权宜之计——一种实用工具，而非道德权威。当它变得不再实用或 actively 有害时，公民既有权利也有义务抵抗它。

关键论点与摘录

论政府的有限价值

"最好的政府是管得最少的政府……最好的政府是根本不管理的政府"

梭罗认为政府"充其量只是一种权宜之计"，并将常备政府比作常备军，都是值得怀疑的对象。

"它不能保持国家的自由。它不能开拓西部。它不能教育人民。美国人民固有的品格才完成了所有已取得的成就"

论多数统治 vs 良心

"我们首先应该是人，其次才是臣民"

  • 多数统治得以持续，不是因为多数是对的，而是因为他们是身体上最强壮的
  • 一个由多数决定对错的国家"不可能建立在正义的基础上"
  • 良心，而非权宜之计，应该支配道德决策

"我唯一有权承担的义务，就是在任何时候做我认为正确的事"

论通过服务成为同谋

梭罗区分了三种为国家服务的方式：

类型方式例子道德地位
身体的仆人体力劳动，不做道德判断士兵、民兵、狱卒、警官"与马和狗同等价值"
头脑的仆人智力服务，不做道德区分立法者、政治家、律师、牧师"同样可能为魔鬼服务……也为上帝服务"
良心的仆人道德上抵抗国家英雄、爱国者、殉道者、改革者被国家视为敌人

"一个智者只会作为一个人有用，而不会屈从于成为'泥土'，'堵住洞不让风吹进来'"

论抵抗的紧迫性（墨西哥战争与奴隶制）

"当一个声称要成为自由庇护所的国家的六分之一人口是奴隶，整个国家被外国军队不公正地征服和占领时……我认为正直的人起来反叛和革命并不算太早"

关键点：这种义务更加紧迫，因为"我们是入侵的军队"——这种不公正正在以我们的名义进行。

对佩利权宜论的批判

梭罗拒绝了威廉·佩利关于公民义务源于社会权宜之计的论点：

"如果我不公正地从溺水者手中夺走了木板，我必须把它还给他，即使我自己会淹死"

正义必须得到伸张，"不惜一切代价"——即使以国家存在为代价。

论投票的愚蠢

"所有投票都是一种赌博，像跳棋或双陆棋，带有一点道德色彩"

  • 即使投票支持正确的事，也是无所作为
  • "只有他的投票才能加速奴隶制的废除，他通过投票来宣告自己的自由"

论抗税与个人行动

梭罗的直接行动：拒绝缴纳人头税六年；被监禁一夜（1846年）

"我已经六年没有缴纳人头税了。因为这个原因，我曾经被关进监狱一次，关了一夜"

监狱经历：

  • 观察到"那个机构的愚蠢，它把我当作仅仅是血肉和骨头，要把我锁起来"
  • 感到他"独自一人在所有镇民中缴纳了我的税"
  • 国家"从未有意面对一个人的理智或道德，而只面对他的身体"

"我看到，如果我和镇民之间有一堵石墙，还有一堵更难以攀爬或打破的墙，在他们能像我一样自由之前"

论打破不公正的法律

"如果[不公正]的性质要求你成为对另一个人不公正的代理人，那么，我说，打破法律。让你的生命成为阻止机器的反向摩擦"

"我要做的是，无论如何，不要让自己成为我谴责的错误的帮凶"

论正义者的真正位置

"在一个不公正地监禁任何人的政府下，正义者的真正位置也是监狱"

"在奴隶州中，自由人能够体面地居住的唯一房子"

和平革命的定义："如果一千人今年不缴纳他们的税单，这不会是一种暴力和血腥的措施，就像缴纳它们一样，使国家能够实施暴力和杀害无辜者"

论改革的局限

梭罗区分了空谈与行动：

"改革让许多报纸为其服务，但没有一个男人"

"有九百九十九个美德的赞助人，只有一个有德的人"

论财产与道德基础

"绝对地说，钱越多，美德越少"

  • 富人"总是卖给使他富有的机构"
  • 金钱"让许多他本来会烦恼的问题安息"

论个人的力量

"我知道得很清楚，如果一千人，如果我可以说出一百人的名字，如果十个人，——是的，如果只有一个诚实的人，在这个马萨诸塞州，停止拥有奴隶，实际上退出这种合作关系，并因此被关进县监狱，那将是美国奴隶制的废除"

On the Duty of Civil Disobedience

Original title: Resistance to Civil Government

Core Thesis

Government is fundamentally an expedient—a practical tool, not a moral authority. When it becomes inexpedient or actively harmful, citizens have both the right and duty to resist it.

Key Arguments & Excerpts

On Government's Limited Value

"That government is best which governs least... That government is best which governs not at all"

Thoreau argues government is "at best but an expedient" and compares standing governments to standing armies as objects of justified suspicion.

"It does not keep the country free. It does not settle the West. It does not educate. The character inherent in the American people has done all that has been accomplished"

On Majority Rule vs. Conscience

"We should be men first, and subjects afterward"

  • Majority rule persists not because majorities are right, but because they are physically strongest
  • A government where majorities decide right and wrong "cannot be based on justice"
  • Conscience, not expediency, should govern moral decisions

"The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right"

On Complicity Through Service

Thoreau distinguishes three modes of serving the State:

TypeMethodExampleMoral Status
Body servantsPhysical labor, no moral judgmentSoldiers, militia, jailers, constables"Same worth as horses and dogs"
Head servantsIntellectual service without moral distinctionLegislators, politicians, lawyers, ministers"As likely to serve the devil... as God"
Conscience servantsMoral resistance to StateHeroes, patriots, martyrs, reformersTreated as enemies by the State

"A wise man will only be useful as a man, and will not submit to be 'clay,' and 'stop a hole to keep the wind away'"

On the Urgency of Resistance (Mexican War & Slavery)

"When a sixth of the population of a nation which has undertaken to be the refuge of liberty are slaves, and a whole country is unjustly overrun and conquered by a foreign army... I think that it is not too soon for honest men to rebel and revolutionize"

Critical point: The duty is more urgent because "ours is the invading army"—the injustice is being done in our name.

Critique of Paley's Expediency

Thoreau rejects William Paley's argument that civil obligation derives from societal expediency:

"If I have unjustly wrested a plank from a drowning man, I must restore it to him though I drown myself"

Justice must be done "cost what it may"—even at the price of national existence.

On the Folly of Voting

"All voting is a sort of gaming, like chequers or backgammon, with a slight moral tinge to it"

  • Even voting for the right is doing nothing for it
  • "Only his vote can hasten the abolition of slavery who asserts his own freedom by his vote"

On Tax Resistance & Personal Action

Thoreau's direct action: Refused poll-tax for six years; imprisoned for one night (1846)

"I have paid no poll-tax for six years. I was put into a jail once on this account, for one night"

The prison experience:

  • Observed the "foolishness of that institution which treated me as if I were mere flesh and blood and bones, to be locked up"
  • Felt he "alone of all my townsmen had paid my tax"
  • The State "never intentionally confronts a man's sense, intellectual or moral, but only his body"

"I saw that, if there was a wall of stone between me and my townsmen, there was a still more difficult one to climb or break through, before they could get to be as free as I was"

On Breaking Unjust Laws

"If [the injustice] is of such a nature that it requires you to be the agent of injustice to another, then, I say, break the law. Let your life be a counter friction to stop the machine"

"What I have to do is to see, at any rate, that I do not lend myself to the wrong which I condemn"

On the True Place for a Just Man

"Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison"

"The only house in a slave-state in which a free man can abide with honor"

Peaceable revolution defined: "If a thousand men were not to pay their tax-bills this year, that would not be a violent and bloody measure, as it would be to pay them, and enable the State to commit violence and shed innocent blood"

On the Limits of Reform

Thoreau distinguishes talk from action:

"Reform keeps many scores of newspapers in its service, but not one man"

"There are nine hundred and ninety-nine patrons of virtue to one virtuous man"

On Property and Moral Ground

"Absolutely speaking, the more money, the less virtue"

  • The rich are "always sold to the institution which makes him rich"
  • Money "puts to rest many questions which he would otherwise be vexed with"

On the Individual's Power

"I know this well, that if one thousand, if one hundred, if ten men whom I could name,—if ten honest men only,—aye, if one HONEST man, in this State of Massachusetts, ceasing to hold slaves, were actually to withdraw from this copartnership, and be locked up in the county jail therefor, it would be the abolition of slavery in America"
